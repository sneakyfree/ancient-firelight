 EP01 — TELEPROMPTER
 "What Did Ancient Humans Do at Night?"

Read at a relaxed pace (~150 words/min ≈ 10 min). Section headers are silent cues for you, not spoken. `[ pause ]` = beat. Blank lines = breath. Nothing here is a stage direction to read aloud.

 — COLD OPEN —

The sun goes down.

And for the next twelve hours, you can't work. You can't forage, you can't hunt, you can't dig, you can't walk anywhere that isn't memorized. You can barely see your own hands.

For two hundred thousand years, that was half of your life. Half.

So what did you do?

You already know the answer, because you've seen it in every documentary ever made. You huddled around a fire, because fire kept the animals away. And that's where we started telling stories.

That's three claims.

[ pause ]

Two of them have no evidence. Not thin evidence. Not contested evidence. None.

And the one that's left standing is so much stranger than the story that I genuinely don't understand why anyone bothered making the story up.


 — PART ONE — THE ANIMALS THAT WEREN'T THERE —

Start with the predators, because this one's almost funny.

The idea is everywhere: night was lethal, big cats hunted in the dark, so we built fires to hold them off, and the fire made us human. You'll find it in textbooks. You'll find it in a Nature paper from this year.

So I went looking for the study. The one that shows fire keeps predators away.

There isn't one.

There is a real study about lions and darkness, and it's excellent. Craig Packer's team went through more than a thousand people attacked by lions in Tanzania, over twenty-one years. And they found something genuinely chilling: attacks spike two to four times in the ten days after a full moon — when the evening goes dark early.

Nights are dangerous. That part's true, and it's measured.

But here's the second sentence of that paper's own abstract. Not my summary — theirs:

That study is about moonlight. It contains no fire data whatsoever.

Watch what happens next, because this is how a fact gets made out of nothing.

A major review cites Packer to establish that nights were dangerous — which he did show. And then it takes one step sideways: "Control of fire is the obvious possibility." Obvious. Not demonstrated. Obvious.

And then the next paper cites that review — and by the time it comes out the other side, "protection from predators" is just sitting there in the abstract, like a fact, wearing a little hat.

Nobody lied. Every single person hedged honestly. It's just that hedges don't survive citation.

And here's the part that should have ended this a long time ago. The Hadza still sleep on the ground in lion country in Tanzania. Researchers watched four hundred and thirty-six of their nights.

They ran a fire on two hundred and ninety-five of them.

Sixty-seven percent. One night in three, no fire. In lion country.

And if you want to know what people do in 2026 when they actually, urgently need to stop lions from killing their livestock at night — there's a review of every method tried over twenty-six years. Reinforced enclosures. Guard dogs. Shock collars. Flashing lights.

Fire isn't on the list.


 — PART TWO — YOU ARE NOT TIRED BECAUSE OF ELECTRICITY —

Second claim. The one you've felt in your bones at 2 a.m.: that our ancestors slept properly, long and deep and whole, and then we invented the light bulb and ruined ourselves.

So somebody went and measured it. Three societies without electricity — the Hadza in Tanzania, the San in Namibia, the Tsimane in Bolivia. Ninety-four people. Eleven hundred and sixty-five nights.

They sleep five point seven to seven point one hours.

That's you. That's your Tuesday. That's the amount of sleep you feel guilty about.

They wake up in the night too — about eighty minutes' worth, on average. They're not sleeping some lost, perfect sleep.

But here's the twist, and it's the good kind. Chronic insomnia runs at ten to thirty percent in industrial societies. Among these groups, the researchers put it at one and a half to two and a half percent.

Two of the languages don't have a word for it.

So it isn't that they get more sleep. It's that sleep isn't a problem. It isn't a thing you fail at. Nobody there is lying awake at 3 a.m. calculating how many hours they have left before the alarm — which is, I'd argue, the single most modern activity a human being can perform.

The researchers' own conclusion, and I want to read it exactly, because it torches an entire industry:

Now, you may have heard we used to sleep in two shifts. First sleep, wake for an hour, second sleep. That's real history — Roger Ekirch found thousands of references to it in old European texts, and he's defended it hard for twenty years.

The equatorial groups don't do it. But a non-electric farming community in Madagascar does — segmented, straight out of the historical record.

So the fight's still live, and the best answer on the table is beautifully simple: it might just be night length. Twelve-hour equatorial nights, you sleep once. Fourteen-hour European winter nights, you split.

Not a lost human birthright. Just how much dark you were handed.


 — PART THREE — WHAT'S ACTUALLY THERE —

So. No predator evidence. No lost golden sleep.

Which leaves the third claim — the stories. And this one is real, and it's measured, and it's the best number I've found in years.

In 1974, an anthropologist named Polly Wiessner sat down with the Ju/'hoansi in Botswana and did something almost nobody does: she wrote down what people talked about. By day, and by night. A hundred and seventy-four conversations.

Daytime, people talked about:

Complaints. Criticism. Who's not pulling their weight. Thirty-four percent.

Economics. Foraging plans, who's going where, what's ripe. Thirty-one percent.

Jokes — sixteen. Stories? About six percent.

Now the sun goes down.

Stories: eighty-one percent.

Complaints collapse to seven. Economics to four.

Same people. Same day. Same mouths. The sun goes down and they stop being colleagues and start being a culture.

Two things, honestly, before anyone screams in the comments — and you'd be right to.

That eighty-one percent is from the long conversations, the big group ones, and she went looking for those. And her fieldwork ran through an unusually dry, hungry stretch. She lists both. She lists five other limitations too. She's the one who flagged them.


 — THE PAYLOAD — THE NIGHT WATCH NOBODY POSTED —

And then there's the fact that stopped me cold.

Back to the Hadza. Thirty-three people, wired up, twenty nights. And the researchers went looking for a simple thing: how often is everybody asleep at the same time?

Out of twenty nights — out of tens of thousands of minutes — the number of moments when every single person was asleep at once was eighteen.

Eighteen minutes. In twenty nights.

Someone was awake ninety-nine point eight percent of the time. Usually about eight people.

Now — nobody organised this. There's no rota. Nobody's on watch. Ask them and they'd tell you they're just sleeping.

It happens because chronotype drifts with age. Old people wake early. Young people go to bed late. Put three generations around one fire and the coverage is total, automatically, forever, without a single person intending it.

Your grandmother isn't a bad sleeper.

She's the night watch.

And that is the thing I want you to take out of here — because it's the shape of this whole channel.

Nobody designed that. There was no plan, no chief, no meeting. Just a bunch of ordinary bodies with slightly different clocks, and out the other end comes a solution better than one you'd have engineered.

That's not a story about sleep. That's how almost everything about us got built.


 — CLOSE —

So — what did ancient humans do at night?

They slept about as badly as you do. They probably weren't fighting off lions with sticks of fire, and if they were, nobody's ever managed to show it.

What they did was talk. And the only reason we can say that is that somebody bothered to count, in one place, fifty-two nights, in 1974.

And here's where I have to stop, because Wiessner does. She's blunt about it: "Data from modern foragers cannot be projected back in the past." She said it plainer to a reporter — "We can't tell about the past from the Bushmen."

She's right. I'm not going to tell you this is where the human mind was born, because I can't know that, and neither can anyone selling you that sentence.

But I'll tell you what the night is, structurally, and this part isn't a theory — it's geometry. It's the only stretch of the day you cannot work. You can't forage in the dark. You can't gather. The fire's not good enough light to make anything decent.

It's the only time you're no use to anybody.

And what happens in the hours when you're no use to anybody — six percent, versus eighty-one — is that you stop dealing with each other and you start telling each other.

Two hundred thousand years of that.

If you've ever noticed you're a different person at eleven at night than at eleven in the morning — softer, stranger, more honest, more likely to say the true thing —

that's not a modern problem.

That's the oldest room we have.

Next time: somebody pressed their hand against a wall sixty-seven thousand eight hundred years ago — and filed the fingertips into claws first.
